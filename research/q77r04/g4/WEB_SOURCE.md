# PROVED

For the stated left-coset action,
\[
\rho=4,\qquad Q_*=\frac12I+\frac18A,
\]
and there is a measurable \(\Gamma\)-equivariant map
\[
F:[0,1]^W\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^W
\]
whose pushforward of product Lebesgue measure is \(\mathbf P^{Q_*}\).

The construction below uses a uniformly Lipschitz posterior drift and a same-noise integral equation. It works at the specified endpoint.

## 1. Cosets, the operator, and the Bass–Serre tree

The HNN normal-form lemma gives
\[
H\cap tHt^{-1}=\langle a^3\rangle,
\qquad
H\cap t^{-1}Ht=\langle a^2\rangle.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, \(ta^kt^{-1}\in H\) requires \(2\mid k\), in which case it equals \(a^{3k/2}\). Similarly, \(t^{-1}a^kt\in H\) requires \(3\mid k\), and then equals \(a^{2k/3}\).

Thus the operator in the question is
\[
T\delta_{gH}=\sum_{j=0}^{2}\delta_{ga^jtH}.
\tag{2}
\]
The three cosets are distinct, since
\[
a^itH=a^jtH\iff a^{i-j}\in\langle a^3\rangle.
\]
Changing \(g\) to \(ga^m\) permutes these three cosets, so the definition is independent of the representative of \(gH\). Left multiplication plainly commutes with \(T\).

Reversing the adjacency relation gives
\[
T^*\delta_{gH}=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\delta_{ga^jt^{-1}H}.
\tag{3}
\]
These are the left-coset/right-Hecke conventions of Anantharaman-Delaroche, Section 1: the column and row subdegrees are respectively \(L(t)=3\) and \(R(t)=2\). :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

Write \(x\to y\) when \(\delta_y\) occurs in \(T\delta_x\). Each vertex has three outgoing and two incoming neighbors. Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|Tf\|_2^2
&=\sum_y\left|\sum_{x:x\to y}f(x)\right|^2\\
&\le 2\sum_y\sum_{x:x\to y}|f(x)|^2
=6\|f\|_2^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence \(T\) is bounded. At \(o=H\),
\[
\tau(T^*T)=\|T\delta_o\|^2=3,
\qquad
\tau(TT^*)=\|T^*\delta_o\|^2=2.
\tag{4}
\]
In particular, \(\tau\) is not a trace.

To identify the graph, take positive edges to be
\[
\Gamma/\langle a^3\rangle,
\]
with \(g\langle a^3\rangle\) directed from \(gH\) to \(gtH\). This is well-defined because \(a^{3k}t=ta^{2k}\). The graph is connected because \(\Gamma\) is generated by \(H\) and \(t\). A nonbacktracking closed path would give an HNN-reduced word containing stable letters but belonging to \(H\), contradicting the normal-form lemma. Thus it is a tree.

This is precisely the Bass–Serre construction in Anantharaman-Delaroche, Example 2.5, using associated subgroups \(\langle a^3\rangle,\langle a^2\rangle\) in her convention \(t^{-1}ht=\sigma(h)\). Its underlying unoriented graph is the **5-regular tree**, and \(A=T+T^*\) is ordinary adjacency. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

For completeness, the action’s actual modular ratio is
\[
\Delta(x,y)
=\frac{[\Gamma_y:\Gamma_x\cap\Gamma_y]}
       {[\Gamma_x:\Gamma_x\cap\Gamma_y]}
=\left(\frac23\right)^{\operatorname{ht}(y)-\operatorname{ht}(x)},
\tag{5}
\]
where \(\operatorname{ht}(gH)\) is the exponent sum of \(t\) in \(g\). On a positive edge, the numerator and denominator are \(2\) and \(3\). Multiplicativity follows by computing indices over a common finite-index subgroup. No mass-transport identity will be used below.

## 2. Derivation of \(\rho\) and endpoint positivity

Root the unoriented tree temporarily at \(o\). For an edge with parent \(p\) and child \(q\),
\[
2|f(p)f(q)|\le \frac12|f(p)|^2+2|f(q)|^2.
\]
After summing over edges, each nonroot vertex receives coefficient
\[
4\cdot\frac12+2=4,
\]
while the root receives \(5/2\). Therefore
\[
|\langle Af,f\rangle|\le4\|f\|_2^2,
\]
so \(\|A\|\le4\).

For \(R\ge1\), set
\[
f_R(x)=2^{-d(o,x)}\mathbf1_{\{d(o,x)\le R\}}.
\]
The sphere at distance \(k\ge1\) has \(5\cdot4^{k-1}\) vertices. Consequently,
\[
\|f_R\|_2^2=1+\frac54R,
\qquad
\langle Af_R,f_R\rangle=5R.
\]
Thus
\[
\frac{\langle Af_R,f_R\rangle}{\|f_R\|_2^2}
=\frac{20R}{4+5R}\longrightarrow4,
\]
proving
\[
\boxed{\rho=4.}
\tag{6}
\]

The bipartite sign unitary \(Jf(x)=(-1)^{d(o,x)}f(x)\) satisfies \(JAJ=-A\). Both \(4\) and \(-4\) therefore belong to the spectrum. Hence
\[
Q_*=\frac12I+\frac18A,\qquad 0\le Q_*\le I,
\tag{7}
\]
and \(0,1\in\sigma(Q_*)\): there is no spectral gap at either endpoint.

Since \(W\) is countable and \(Q_*\) is a positive contraction, its DPP exists, with finite inclusion probabilities
\[
\mu\{\eta_x=1\text{ for }x\in S\}
=\det((Q_*)|_S),
\qquad \mu:=\mathbf P^{Q_*}.
\tag{8}
\]
This is the countable-set DPP existence statement in Lyons–Thom, Section 2; its hypotheses have now been checked. Invariance of \(Q_*\) implies invariance of \(\mu\). :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

## 3. A finite-posterior Lipschitz estimate

The sampling mechanism is an observation/innovation construction, related to the Brownian-observation method used by Nam–Sly–Zhang for the Ising model. Their Ising theorem is not being applied here; the required DPP estimate and infinite-volume construction follow directly. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

**Lemma.** Let \(\nu\) be a DPP on a finite set \(E\), with Hermitian kernel \(0\le K\le I\). Define its positive external-field tilt by
\[
\nu_h(\sigma)=
\frac{\nu(\sigma)e^{\sum_{z\in E}h_z\sigma_z}}
{\sum_\xi\nu(\xi)e^{\sum_{z\in E}h_z\xi_z}},
\qquad
m_x(h)=\mathbb E_{\nu_h}\sigma_x.
\]
Then
\[
\sum_{y\in E}|\partial_{h_y}m_x(h)|\le\frac12,
\qquad
|m_x(h)-m_x(h')|
\le\frac12\|h-h'\|_\infty.
\tag{9}
\]

**Proof.** We first verify closure under tilting without assuming \(K\) or \(I-K\) invertible. Put
\[
D=\operatorname{diag}(e^{h_z}),\qquad
B=D^{1/2}K^{1/2},\qquad
M=I-K+B^*B.
\]
Then
\[
M\ge \min(1,\min_z e^{h_z})I>0.
\]
Define
\[
P=BM^{-1}B^*.
\tag{10}
\]
It is positive, and \(M\ge B^*B\) implies \(BM^{-1/2}\) is a contraction, so \(P\le I\).

For diagonal \(Z=\operatorname{diag}(z_x)\), the DPP generating polynomial is
\[
\mathbb E_\nu\prod_x z_x^{\sigma_x}
=\det\!\left(I+K^{1/2}(Z-I)K^{1/2}\right).
\]
This follows by expanding \(\prod_x[1+(z_x-1)\sigma_x]\) and using the inclusion determinants. Therefore the tilted generating polynomial is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\nu_h}\prod_xz_x^{\sigma_x}
&=\frac{\det(M+B^*(Z-I)B)}{\det M}\\
&=\det(I+(Z-I)BM^{-1}B^*)\\
&=\det(I+(Z-I)P).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the tilted law is a Hermitian-contraction DPP with kernel \(P\).

Differentiating the finite normalized sum gives
\[
\partial_{h_y}m_x(h)
=\operatorname{Cov}_{\nu_h}(\sigma_x,\sigma_y).
\]
Writing \(p=P_{xx}\), the one- and two-point determinants give
\[
\operatorname{Cov}_{\nu_h}(\sigma_x,\sigma_y)=
\begin{cases}
p(1-p),&y=x,\\
-|P_{xy}|^2,&y\ne x.
\end{cases}
\]
Since \(P^2\le P\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_y|\partial_{h_y}m_x(h)|
&=p(1-p)+(P^2)_{xx}-p^2\\
&\le2p(1-p)\le\frac12.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]
Integration along the segment from \(h\) to \(h'\) proves (9). \(\square\)

This is a **single-row finite-matrix estimate**, not a trace or an averaged mass-transport identity.

## 4. A globally defined equivariant drift

Let \(E_R(x)\) be the radius-\(R\) ball around \(x\) in the unoriented tree. Let \(\mu_E\) denote the actual marginal of \(\mu\) on \(E\), whose kernel is \((Q_*)|_E\).

For \(s\ge0\) and \(y\in\mathbb R^W\), define
\[
b^R_{s,x}(y)=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{E_R(x)}}
\sigma_x\mu_{E_R(x)}(\sigma)
\exp\!\left(\sum_{z\in E_R(x)}(y_z-s/2)\sigma_z\right)}
{
\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{E_R(x)}}
\mu_{E_R(x)}(\sigma)
\exp\!\left(\sum_{z\in E_R(x)}(y_z-s/2)\sigma_z\right)}.
\tag{12}
\]
All denominators are positive. Define, on **every** observation field,
\[
b_{s,x}(y)=\limsup_{R\to\infty}b^R_{s,x}(y).
\tag{13}
\]

Each finite expression is jointly Borel in \((s,y)\), takes values in \([0,1]\), and depends on finitely many coordinates. Thus \(b\) is jointly Borel and bounded. Invariance of \(\mu\) and equivariance of the balls give
\[
b_s(\gamma y)=\gamma b_s(y),
\qquad
(\gamma y)_x=y_{\gamma^{-1}x}.
\]

By (9), whenever \(y-y'\) is bounded,
\[
\sup_x|b_{s,x}(y)-b_{s,x}(y')|
\le\frac12\sup_z|y_z-y'_z|.
\tag{14}
\]
Indeed, the inequality holds before taking either limsup.

Neither field itself needs to be bounded. The limsup also avoids an important version problem: it defines the drift even on fields outside the observation law, where Picard iterates might lie. No infinite-volume tilted kernel or convergence on every field is assumed.

## 5. An explicit pathwise construction from one noise field

Write
\[
\mathcal W=C_0([0,\infty),\mathbb R)^W,
\]
where \(C_0\) denotes continuous paths starting at zero. For an arbitrary \(w\in\mathcal W\), consider
\[
Z_t(x)=w_t(x)+\int_0^t b_{s,x}(Z_s)\,ds.
\tag{15}
\]

Construct it by
\[
Z^{(0)}=w,\qquad
Z^{(n+1)}_t(x)
=w_t(x)+\int_0^t b_{s,x}(Z^{(n)}_s)\,ds.
\tag{16}
\]
These are coordinatewise Lebesgue integrals of bounded Borel functions.

Induction using (14) gives
\[
\sup_x|Z^{(n+1)}_t(x)-Z^{(n)}_t(x)|
\le\frac{2^{-n}t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
\tag{17}
\]
The initial bound is \(t\); each induction step integrates one half of the previous bound.

For every \(T<\infty\), the right-hand sides, with \(t\) replaced by \(T\), are summable in \(n\). Therefore \(Z^{(n)}-w\) converges uniformly over \(W\times[0,T]\). Every limit coordinate is continuous, and (14) permits passage through the integrals. Thus the limit solves (15).

Every iterate is a Borel function of \((t,w)\), so the resulting path map
\[
\mathcal Z:\mathcal W\to\mathcal W
\]
is Borel. Every iterate and its limit are equivariant.

There is also uniqueness for the same input \(w\). Any solution has
\[
0\le Z_t(x)-w_t(x)\le t.
\]
For two solutions, \(D(t)=\sup_x|Z_t(x)-Z'_t(x)|\) is finite and satisfies
\[
D(t)\le\frac12\int_0^tD(s)\,ds.
\]
Starting with \(D(s)\le s\), iteration gives arbitrarily high factorial bounds, forcing \(D(t)=0\).

This argument uses bounded **differences** of fields. It does not assume that an iid Brownian field is an \(\ell^\infty(W)\)-valued random variable.

## 6. Exact identification of the constructed path law

The following auxiliary coupling is used only to identify the law; it is not additional input to the factor.

Take
\[
\eta\sim\mu,
\]
and independent standard Brownian motions \(B(x)\), \(x\in W\), independent of \(\eta\). Define
\[
X_t(x)=t\eta_x+B_t(x).
\tag{18}
\]
Let
\[
\mathcal F_t^0
=\sigma(X_r(x):0\le r\le t,\ x\in W).
\]

### Finite and infinite posteriors

For fixed \(t>0\), conditional on \(\eta|_E=\sigma\), the Gaussian density of \(X_t|_E\), after removing factors independent of \(\sigma\), is
\[
\exp\!\left(
\sum_{z\in E}X_t(z)\sigma_z-\frac t2\sum_{z\in E}\sigma_z
\right).
\tag{19}
\]
Here \(\sigma_z^2=\sigma_z\), explaining the necessary \(-t/2\) in (12).

Bayes’ formula therefore gives
\[
b^R_{t,x}(X_t)
=\mathbb E[\eta_x\mid X_t(z),\,z\in E_R(x)].
\tag{20}
\]
The conditioning sigma-fields increase to \(\sigma(X_t(z):z\in W)\). Since \(\eta_x\) is bounded, the upward conditional-expectation convergence theorem applies, giving
\[
b_{t,x}(X_t)
=\mathbb E[\eta_x\mid X_t(z),\,z\in W]
\quad\text{almost surely}.
\tag{21}
\]
Thus along the observation process the limsup is an actual limit at every fixed time and vertex. These are precisely the integrability and increasing-sigma-field hypotheses of Lévy’s upward theorem. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

The observation past supplies no further information about \(\eta\) beyond the endpoints. Indeed,
\[
X_r(x)-\frac rtX_t(x)
=B_r(x)-\frac rtB_t(x),\qquad 0\le r\le t.
\]
These Brownian bridges are jointly independent of
\[
\bigl(\eta,(X_t(z))_{z\in W}\bigr).
\]
This follows from finite-dimensional Gaussian independence and then passage to the countable sigma-fields. The bridges and endpoints generate \(\mathcal F_t^0\), so
\[
b_{t,x}(X_t)=\mathbb E[\eta_x\mid\mathcal F_t^0]
\quad\text{almost surely}.
\tag{22}
\]

These identities are asserted at each fixed time. Joint measurability and boundedness allow Fubini in all time integrals below; no uncountable intersection of probability-one events is being assumed.

### The innovations are independent Brownian paths

Define
\[
\beta_t(x)
=X_t(x)-\int_0^t b_{s,x}(X_s)\,ds.
\tag{23}
\]
For \(0\le u<v\), Brownian independence, (22), and the tower property give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\beta_v(x)-\beta_u(x)\mid\mathcal F_u^0]
&=(v-u)\mathbb E[\eta_x\mid\mathcal F_u^0]\\
&\quad-\int_u^v
\mathbb E[b_{s,x}(X_s)\mid\mathcal F_u^0]\,ds\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The Brownian increment has zero conditional expectation because
\[
\mathcal F_u^0
\subseteq\sigma(\eta,B_r(z):r\le u,\ z\in W).
\]

Thus each \(\beta(x)\) is a continuous square-integrable martingale, starting at zero. Since \(\beta(x)-B(x)\) has continuous finite variation,
\[
[\beta(x),\beta(y)]_t=\mathbf1_{\{x=y\}}t.
\tag{24}
\]

The martingale property passes to the usual right-continuous completion of the filtration: condition on \(\mathcal F_{s+1/n}^0\) and use decreasing conditional-expectation convergence and \(L^1\)-continuity of \(\beta\).

For any finite list of distinct vertices, the resulting vector martingale has bracket \(tI\). Lévy’s characterization therefore gives standard vector Brownian motion. Explicitly, for every \(\theta\in\mathbb R^k\), Itô’s formula makes
\[
\exp\!\left(i\theta\cdot\beta_t+\frac12|\theta|^2t\right)
\]
a martingale on bounded time intervals. Its modulus is deterministically bounded there, so it is a true martingale. Conditional characteristic functions of increments are consequently
\[
\exp\!\left(-\frac12|\theta|^2(t-s)\right).
\]
This proves independent Gaussian increments and independence of the coordinate paths, not merely zero covariance. The continuity, initial-value, bracket, and filtration hypotheses of the characterization have all been verified. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

Since \(W\) is countable,
\[
(\beta(x))_{x\in W}
\quad\text{has product Wiener law}.
\tag{25}
\]

Equation (23) says that \(X\) solves (15) with input \(\beta\). Pathwise uniqueness from Section 5 gives
\[
X=\mathcal Z(\beta)
\quad\text{almost surely}.
\tag{26}
\]
Therefore applying \(\mathcal Z\) to iid Brownian paths on \(W\) produces exactly the observation-process law (18). No infinite-product change of measure is used.

## 7. The \(W\)-indexed Uniform factor and its same-iid limit

Fix an everywhere Borel map
\[
\Psi:[0,1]\to C_0([0,\infty),\mathbb R)
\]
that sends Lebesgue measure to Wiener measure.

Such a map can be constructed directly from one label. Split its binary digits into countably many independent Uniform variables, and transform these into independent standard normals. Use fresh normals for successive unit-interval increments and, recursively, assign a dyadic midpoint on \([u,v]\) the value
\[
\frac{B(u)+B(v)}2+\frac{\sqrt{v-u}}2G.
\]
At level \(k\), the maximum change in the interpolant is at most
\[
2^{-k/2-1}\max_{j<2^k}|G_{k,j}|.
\]
The Gaussian union bound shows that the maximum is at most \(k+1\) eventually almost surely. Hence the interpolants converge locally uniformly. Gaussian conditioning verifies Brownian finite-dimensional distributions. Assign the zero path on the Borel null exceptional set. This gives the required \(\Psi\).

Now set
\[
w(x)=\Psi(U_x),\qquad x\in W.
\]
This uses exactly one Uniform label at each vertex, with the same encoding at every vertex.

For a path field \(w\), define
\[
G_n(w)_x
=\mathbf1_{\{\mathcal Z(w)_n(x)>n/2\}},
\qquad
G(w)_x=\liminf_{n\to\infty}G_n(w)_x.
\tag{27}
\]
Finally put
\[
F(U)=G\bigl((\Psi(U_x))_{x\in W}\bigr).
\tag{28}
\]

Every operation is measurable and equivariant. The binary liminf defines the output even on exceptional inputs. Thus \(F\) is an everywhere-defined measurable \(\Gamma\)-equivariant map.

It remains to prove convergence and its exact law. In the coupling (18), (23), and (26), conditional on either value of \(\eta_x\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P\{G_n(\beta)_x\ne\eta_x\}
&=\mathbb P\{N(0,1)>\sqrt n/2\}\\
&\le e^{-n/8}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{29}
\]
A Gaussian tie has probability zero.

The error probabilities are summable. Borel–Cantelli implies that, for each vertex, \(G_n(\beta)_x\) is eventually equal to \(\eta_x\). Countability of \(W\) gives this simultaneously at every vertex on one probability-one event. A common finite stabilization time for all vertices is not required.

Crucially, the **same** input \(\beta\) is used for every \(n\), and it has product Wiener law. In particular,
\[
\mathbb P\{G_n(\beta)_x\ne G_{n+1}(\beta)_x\}
\le e^{-n/8}+e^{-(n+1)/8}.
\tag{30}
\]
Thus this is a same-iid limit, not merely weak convergence of laws. For a finite set \(S\), the coupling also gives disagreement probability at most \(|S|e^{-n/8}\) between \(G_n(\beta)|_S\) and \(\eta|_S\).

We have proved \(G(\beta)=\eta\) almost surely. Combining this with (25) and the Uniform-to-Wiener encoding proves
\[
F_*(\mathrm{Leb}^{\otimes W})=\mu=\mathbf P^{Q_*}.
\]

In particular, for every finite \(S\subset W\),
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb P\{F(U)_x=1\text{ for all }x\in S\}
=
\det\!\left[
\frac12\mathbf1_{\{x=y\}}
+\frac18\mathbf1_{\{x\sim y\}}
\right]_{x,y\in S}.
}
\tag{31}
\]
For disjoint finite \(S_1,S_0\), the complete pattern probabilities are
\[
\mathbb P\{F|_{S_1}=1,\ F|_{S_0}=0\}
=
\sum_{J\subseteq S_0}(-1)^{|J|}
\det\bigl((Q_*)|_{S_1\cup J}\bigr).
\tag{32}
\]

These are exactly the required DPP finite marginals.

The endpoint is included: the only inverse in the posterior lemma is \(M^{-1}\), whose positivity was proved without inverting \(K\) or \(I-K\). The controlling inequality is the fixed-coordinate identity \((P^2)_{xx}\le P_{xx}\), not an unweighted mass-transport argument. There is no remaining endpoint obligation. No finite coding-radius claim is needed for the requested measurable factor. \(\square\)
